The King's Reflection

There was once a king whose heart was slowly turning to stone, and no physician in the land could stay its progress. The king therefore sent a decree to all corners of his kingdom, and promised his daughter's hand and half the kingdom to the one who could cure him. Now, in a poor village there lived a stonemason's son, who knew the ways of stone better than any man alive. When the decree reached his village, the youth put down his tools, and set off for the king's palace. He travelled for seven days over unbeaten paths, and crossed a dark wood where the trees grew so thick that they blocked out the sun. At last he came to a crossroads in a barren field, where a man clad all in black stood waiting. The man said, I know your errand, and I can give you a tool to mend the king's heart. For this service, I ask only for a small thing in return: you must give me your reflection. The youth thought it a small price to win a princess, and so he agreed to the bargain. The man in black gave him a chisel of black iron, and when the youth next looked into a puddle, his reflection had vanished from the still water.
The stonemason's son came at last to the palace, and was brought before the king. He took out the black chisel, placed its tip against the king's chest, and tapped it with his hammer. The stone fell away like dust, and soon the king's heart was a sphere of perfect, smooth grey flesh, but it was cold to the touch. The king felt his chest and nodded, saying, it is fixed, and you shall have your reward. But first, you must perform three tasks to show you are worthy of a kingdom where all things are now in their proper order. You must build for my daughter a staircase of silent water, so that she may walk upon it to her chambers. You must then weave for her a blanket from moonlight, that it might keep her warm without any weight. And last, you must capture the last echo in a jar, so that I may have silence when I please. The princess, who was beautiful, stood beside her father's throne and said nothing. The stonemason's son was then led from the hall and left alone in the courtyard with his three impossible tasks.
The youth went to the palace fountain and struck the flowing water with the black chisel. The water parted for a moment, but it did not hold its shape and flowed on as before. He tried again and again, and at last he sat upon the ground and put his head in his hands. He had no magic of his own and no one to help him, so he prayed to God for a sign. When he finished, he rose and walked until he found a still pool in the palace garden. He knelt and looked into the water, though he knew he would see no face of his own. And it was true he saw no reflection of himself, but instead the face of the king looked back at him, sad and weary. The reflection in the water opened its mouth and said, the heart's true chisel is the heart itself. Then the image of the king rippled and was gone, leaving only the dark water. The youth stayed kneeling by the pool for a long time, not knowing what the words meant, but feeling their weight.
He sat by the water all night, and he repeated the words the reflection had spoken. And as the sun rose, a terrible understanding came to him, for he knew that the king's face appeared where his own should be. The stonemason's son said to himself, I am his reflection, made real by the man in black. His heart became stone because he had no reflection of his own to soften it. He now knew what the words meant: that to shape the world, he must use the stuff of his own heart. So he thought of the king's long sadness, which was now his own, and he wept. And as his tears fell, he touched each one with the tip of the black chisel, and it became a hard crystal step, silent and clear. He worked all day, and by evening he had built a staircase that reached from the courtyard to the princess's window. That night, he stood in the moonlight to begin his second task. He thought of the hope of winning the princess, and he held out his hands to the moon.
He used the black chisel to draw down threads of pure moonlight, and he wove them together on a loom of air. When morning came, he had a blanket as warm as wool but with no weight at all. For the last task, he stood before the princess's empty chamber and took a deep breath. He called her name, though he did not know it, and the word was full of love. As the last whisper of it returned to him, he scooped it from the air with the chisel and placed the sound in a glass jar, where it became a swirl of silver mist. Having done all this, he went to the throne room and showed the king his work. The king saw that it was done, but before he could speak, the youth stepped forward and touched the king's chest, and became a man of light who entered the king's cold heart. A great warmth spread through the king's body, and color returned to his face for the first time in many years. The king now had two reflections, one in the water and one in his heart, and he understood all that the youth had done. And so the king, who was now whole, gave his daughter's hand in marriage, and the kingdom had a ruler with a heart of flesh once more.
