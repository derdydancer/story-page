The Swan Father's Promise

Once upon a time, by the shore of a wide and lonely lake, there lived a kingdom of swans. Among them was a proud Swan Father, who ruled a small reedy patch near the great forest. He had a swan wife and seven cygnets, but their nest had grown cold and their young went unfed. In the center of their nest lay not a swan's egg, but a smooth, dark jewel of an egg that held all their gaze. The Swan Father and his wife watched the strange egg all day. He said to himself that its beauty was more important than the cries of his children. They were content in their foolish watch. And so it was that for many days, the cygnets grew weaker. But one day, a great storm rose from the mountains. A black wind tore the nest from the reeds and cast it into the churning water. The Swan Father was greatly afraid for his children. His wife cried out, "Save my perfect egg!" The Swan Father saw his cygnets scattered and drowning, and he knew his choice was his family or the false treasure. He obeyed the cry of his own heart and dove after his children. So he went on his way to gather his lost brood. After a time, he found his oldest cygnet shivering on a distant bank. The little bird was near death and could barely speak. "Whither can I find help?" asked the Swan Father. "You must find the Sunken Reeds," answered the cygnet, "for only they can heal us now." The cygnet's plea was a sharp key, and so it unlocked the father's resolve. The cygnet gave a final warning, "Do not follow the mother's jewel, but follow the father's duty." Then the Swan Father thanked his child and set off toward the Whispering Marshes. At last, he came to a pond as smooth and dark as glass. He looked into the water and saw not the sky, but the image of the strange and beautiful egg. The air was silent, and the reflection promised a perfect world with no hunger and no storms. He hesitated for only a moment. There stood the memory of his folly, guarding the way forward. He struck the water with his great wing, and the false image shattered into a thousand pieces. Now he had to dive into the cold darkness to find the reeds. He was told he must first gather a thousand pearls from the mud before the moon was full. He knew not what to do, and so he called out, "My children need me, come birds of the air, come fish of the stream!" The minnows he had once fed came, and they worked, and they brought him every pearl from the bottom of the pond. When the spirit of the pond saw this, it knew he was a true father and could not be stopped with selfish tasks. Then it gave him a second task. He must fly through a fog so thick that no light could enter. He flew into the darkness and was soon lost, for he could not tell sky from water. But then he remembered his duty was not to see but to lead. He let out a great trumpeting call, and his lost cygnets heard it in their hearts and called back, guiding him through the fog in the twinkling of an eye. He came then to a great Serpent coiled at the bottom of the pond. "You are strong," hissed the Serpent, "Stay with me in this perfect silence and forget the noisy world." But the Swan Father answered, "My family's noise is the song of my heart." But the Serpent was not satisfied, and set a third task. It demanded the Swan bring it the one thing it could not have: a feather warmed by a father's love. The Swan plucked a downy feather from his own breast, the minnows carried it down through the water, and the memory of his children’s calls guided it to the Serpent. Then the Serpent's anger knew no bounds, for the selfless gift was a poison to its selfish nature. Then the great Serpent uncoiled and lunged at the Swan Father. It wrapped its cold body around him to crush the warmth from his heart. The Swan felt his breath leave him and the light in his eyes grew dim. But with his last strength, he thought not of himself, but of his children waiting for him. He let out one final, mighty call that was not a sound of fear, but a promise to his family. The sound of true fatherhood shattered the Serpent's magic, and so the beast was dissolved into black mud. And in that moment, the dark pond cleared and the Sunken Reeds glowed before him. Then he took the glowing reeds in his beak. The Swan himself was changed, for his eyes no longer held pride, but wisdom. The spirits of the water greatly rejoiced that duty had conquered selfishness. The pond gave him the reeds and clear passage home forever. So he returned to the shore of the wide and lonely lake. He found his wife weeping, for her beautiful egg had cracked, and a voice from the reeds whispered, "The Serpent is dead, and its spell is broken." The wife's foolish obsession was washed away by her tears of regret. And they healed their children and lived happily ever after in their reedy kingdom.
