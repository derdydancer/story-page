The Weaver in the Woods

Once upon a time, there was a king in a rich land. This king had one son, who was of an age to marry. The prince had fine clothes and a good horse, but he did not have a wife. In his father's great stone castle, the prince was safe and comfortable. He spent his days judging the matters of the court. He often thought, "I must find a wife who is clever, but not contrary." He was a dutiful son in all things. And so things went on for a long time. Then one day, the king declared that the prince must choose a bride at once. The first princess was brought, and the prince asked her, "If I gave you a bolt of fine cloth, what would you make?" She replied, "I would take it to the Weaver in the woods, for he pays coin for it, and a husband need never know." The prince sent her away and asked the second princess the very same question. She said, "I would trade it to the Weaver for two bolts of lesser cloth, one for the house and one to sell back to him for my own purse." He sent her away too, and the third princess declared, "Why take your cloth when the Weaver gives me coin for my time? A husband's gift is good, but a secret purse is better." The prince was gravely insulted and told his father, "I will not marry such a wife, but will instead find this Weaver who teaches women such mischief." And so, the prince set off toward the dark woods. After riding for a day, he came to a crossroads where an old woman was gathering sticks. "Whither away, my lord?" asked the old woman. The prince replied honestly, "I seek the miserly Weaver who spoils the hearts of women." The woman nodded and gave him a single, smooth river stone. "Do not speak to him," she said, "but throw this stone at the shuttle of his loom when it flies." Then the prince thanked her, took the stone, and rode on. At last he came to the deepest part of the wood, and in its heart was the Weaver's cottage. The prince took out the stone and threw it with all his might, striking the shuttle in mid-air. The loom fell silent, and a door appeared in the cottage wall where none had been before. The prince stepped inside. There stood the Weaver, a man as thin as a needle and as grey as unwashed wool. The prince saw that the threads of the loom were not wool but shining threads of gold. The Weaver snarled, "You have stopped my work, so now you must do three tasks for me or be my servant forever." First, you must sort this pile of coins into gold, silver, and copper by sunrise; the pile was as large as a haystack. Suddenly, from the shadows crept a thousand mice, who whispered, "You are true of heart, so we shall help you." The mice scurried and sorted, and by morning the coins were in three neat piles. When the Weaver saw this, he scowled, for his plan had failed. But he said, "Now you must do a second task." "You must count every thread in that tapestry before the sun sets." The prince's heart sank, for the threads were finer than hairs and the patterns were like a maze. But then he remembered the stone, and when he held it, each thread began to glow with its own light. In the blink of an eye, the task was done. The prince went to the Weaver and told him the number of threads. The Weaver shrieked, "You have magic! But it will not help you with the final task." The prince stayed silent, remembering the old woman's words. Then the Weaver said, "You must mend the golden shuttle you broke, and you must do it with a thread of your own making." This was the hardest task, for how could a man spin a thread of pure gold? The prince did not know what to do, and the Weaver, seeing his chance, prepared to trap him. The Weaver was filled with rage and abandoned the tasks. He laughed and grabbed a golden thread from his loom to bind the prince. He cast the thread, and it flew like a golden snake. The prince was caught and bound tightly in the golden thread. But the prince, seeing the solution to the final task, twisted the thread that bound him as if he were spinning it. He pulled the thread taut and looped it through the broken shuttle, making it whole again. As the shuttle was mended, the Weaver turned to solid gold, a lifeless statue of greed. The cottage and the loom of disobedience then crumbled into dust. And where the loom had stood, a young woman now appeared, holding a simple spindle of white thread. She was the true spirit of the loom, now freed from the Weaver's curse, and she was beautiful and good. There was great joy between them. The prince knew she was his true bride, and he asked her to be his wife. So the prince returned to his father's castle with his new bride. The three contrary princesses, whose secret purses had turned to dust, confessed their foolishness. The king had them sent home with a warning to be true to their own hearths. And the prince and his new bride ruled the kingdom with wisdom, and they lived happily ever after.
